*Sources are linked throughout and listed at the end. Different organizations count incidents differently, covering different areas and time periods, so I've noted whose numbers are whose.*

A little over a decade ago, Somali piracy looked like a solved problem. In 2026 it's back at levels not seen since 2013.

## How bad it got, and how it ended

At its peak, Somali piracy was a major threat to world shipping:

- **2010:** Somali pirates successfully hijacked 49 ships ([ICC International Maritime Bureau](https://iccwbo.org/news-publications/news/attacks-off-the-somali-coast-drive-piracy-to-record-high-reports-imb/)).
- **2011:** they carried out 237 attacks, about 54% of all piracy attacks worldwide that year (same source).
- **Hostages:** more than 600 seafarers were taken hostage in 2011 ([Gard](https://gard.no/en/insights/somali-piracy-warning/)).
- **Ransoms:** the World Bank estimates pirate groups collected $339 million to $413 million between 2005 and 2012 ([Africa Defense Forum](https://adf-magazine.com/2026/09/pirate-attacks-rise-in-gulf-of-aden/)).

Then it largely stopped. Several things worked together:

- International navies patrolled the area, led by the EU's Operation Atalanta, which began in 2008.
- Ships carried armed guards.
- Shipping companies adopted standard defensive practices, such as razor wire and safe rooms for the crew.

The shipping industry's "High Risk Area" designation for the Indian Ocean was removed in 2023 ([Gard](https://gard.no/en/insights/somali-piracy-warning/)).

## The comeback, by the numbers

Operation Atalanta's count of pirate attacks off the Horn of Africa ([Africa Defense Forum](https://adf-magazine.com/2026/09/pirate-attacks-rise-in-gulf-of-aden/); [Soufan Center](https://thesoufancenter.org/intelbrief-2026-october-6/)):

| Period | Attacks |
|---|---|
| 2013–2019 (seven years combined) | 26 |
| 2020–2022 | 0 |
| 2023 | 6 |
| 2024 | 22 |
| 2025 | 5 |
| **2026 (through mid-September)** | **25** |

That makes 2026 the worst year since 2013, and it isn't over.

**What happened this year:**

- **Early 2026:** the first quarter was quiet, with just two Somali incidents, one of them the hijacking of a dhow ([IMB Q1 report via Japan P&I Club](https://www.piclub.or.jp/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/No.1367_IMB-piracy-and-armed-robbery-report-for-January-March-2026.pdf)).
- **Late April:**
  - On April 21, pirates hijacked the tanker *Honour 25* off the Somali coast. On April 26, about ten pirates in three skiffs seized the cargo ship *Sward* near Garacad ([Castor Vali](https://www.castorvali.com/news/renewed-piracy-threat-somali-basin-gulf-of-aden-2026/)).
  - Between April 21 and May 2 there were six incidents, four of them hijackings ([Windward](https://windward.ai/knowledge-base/somali-piracy-is-back-what-the-incident-data-shows-and-where-the-risk-is-concentrating/)).
  - The Joint Maritime Information Center, which issues threat ratings for the region, raised its rating for Somali waters to "severe" ([Gard](https://gard.no/en/insights/somali-piracy-warning/)).
- **First half of the year:** the IMB attributed ten incidents to Somali pirates, including five hijackings. Somali pirates accounted for **94% of all crew taken hostage worldwide** ([MaritimeCyprus summary of the IMB report](https://maritimecyprus.com/2026/08/03/icc-imb-world-wide-incidents-of-piracy-and-armed-robbery-against-ships-report-from-jan-to-june-2026/)). That stands out because piracy worldwide fell to its lowest first-half level since 1992.
- **Summer:**
  - Pirates used the *Sward* as a mothership, a captured vessel that carries attack boats far out to sea. With it they hijacked other ships, including the Turkish-linked *MV Lutuf*. Somali and Turkish authorities said the *Lutuf* was retaken by force, while Puntland officials said a $2.5 million ransom was paid. The *Sward* was released after 138 days ([Soufan Center](https://thesoufancenter.org/intelbrief-2026-october-6/)).
  - In mid-August, pirates seized a cargo ship about 4 nautical miles off the coast ([Africa Defense Forum](https://adf-magazine.com/2026/09/pirate-attacks-rise-in-gulf-of-aden/)).
- **September:** pirates boarded the merchant ship *Glamor* in the Gulf of Aden. The crew locked themselves in their safe room, and the pirates left when EU, Japanese and Indian forces responded ([Soufan Center](https://thesoufancenter.org/intelbrief-2026-october-6/)).

**How far they reach:** pirates have attacked ships up to 800 nautical miles (about 920 miles) from the Somali coast and boarded vessels with decks 10 meters or more above the water ([Gard](https://gard.no/en/insights/somali-piracy-warning/)).

## Why it's happening

These are the main explanations given by analysts, navies and insurers. Most are well supported; the Houthi link is the least certain.

**1. The warships left.** Starting in late 2023, attacks by Yemen's Houthis on Red Sea shipping pulled naval forces north. After the U.S. and Israeli war with Iran began in February 2026, more warships went to the Strait of Hormuz ([Soufan Center](https://thesoufancenter.org/intelbrief-2026-october-6/)). Windward, a maritime risk-analysis firm, says the Somali Basin now has the thinnest naval coverage it has seen since before Operation Atalanta began in 2008 ([Windward](https://windward.ai/knowledge-base/somali-piracy-is-back-what-the-incident-data-shows-and-where-the-risk-is-concentrating/)). Naval patrols were a large part of what ended piracy the first time, so their absence matters.

**2. More ships are sailing past Somalia.** To avoid the Red Sea and the Gulf, shipping companies are rerouting vessels around Africa and closer to the Somali coast ([Africa Defense Forum](https://adf-magazine.com/2026/09/pirate-attacks-rise-in-gulf-of-aden/)). High oil and gas prices also make tankers especially valuable targets.

**3. The pirate networks never went away.** The Horn Review argues the networks simply kept a low profile. They held on to their know-how, logistics and community ties along the coast, and could start operating again quickly ([Horn Review](https://hornreview.org/2026/05/22/somali-piracy-in-2026-reactivation-of-maritime-risk-in-the-western-indian-ocean/)).

**4. Ransoms still pay.** A Chinese vessel seized in January 2026 was released in March for a reported ransom of up to $1.5 million ([Africa Defense Forum](https://adf-magazine.com/2026/09/pirate-attacks-rise-in-gulf-of-aden/)). Financiers often take 30% to 75% of a ransom, and clan elders and local officials have also benefited. That gives powerful people on shore a reason to protect the business ([Soufan Center](https://thesoufancenter.org/intelbrief-2026-october-6/)).

**5. Poverty, foreign fishing and no coast guard.** Analysts point to:

- poverty and unemployment along the coast;
- foreign trawlers depleting local fishing grounds;
- cuts to U.S. aid programs;
- Somalia's lack of a functioning coast guard.

These are long-standing grievances that helped start piracy in the first place ([Soufan Center](https://thesoufancenter.org/intelbrief-2026-october-6/)). An Operation Atalanta spokesperson cited "the difficult situation for some of the Somali population" as one cause ([Africa Defense Forum](https://adf-magazine.com/2026/09/pirate-attacks-rise-in-gulf-of-aden/)).

**6. A possible link to terrorist groups (less certain).** UN monitoring reports say:

- Somalia's al-Shabaab has stepped up piracy in exchange for weapons and training from the Iran-backed Houthis.
- Al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula has received a share of al-Shabaab's piracy revenue.

([Soufan Center](https://thesoufancenter.org/intelbrief-2026-october-6/))

There are also reports that the Houthis gave pirates GPS equipment. Windward treats that claim as unconfirmed ([Windward](https://windward.ai/knowledge-base/somali-piracy-is-back-what-the-incident-data-shows-and-where-the-risk-is-concentrating/)).

## What to watch

The southwest monsoon usually suppresses piracy from June to August, and analysts warned that activity could surge once it ends ([Soufan Center](https://thesoufancenter.org/intelbrief-2026-october-6/)). The *Glamor* attack in September suggests that's already happening. France and several African and Gulf countries have backed a proposed new counter-piracy task force ([Windward](https://windward.ai/knowledge-base/somali-piracy-is-back-what-the-incident-data-shows-and-where-the-risk-is-concentrating/)).

The pattern so far is that piracy rises when patrols leave and falls when they return. With naval forces tied up in the Red Sea and Hormuz, the late-2026 season will show whether anyone fills the gap.
