*The numbers here come from the CDC, SAMHSA, HRSA, the Commonwealth Fund and peer-reviewed studies. Sources are linked throughout and listed at the end. The last section is my own view on what to do.*

## How big is the problem?

**Mental illness is common, and many people get no treatment.** In 2024, about 61.5 million American adults had a mental illness in the past year. Only 52.1% of them received any mental health treatment ([SAMHSA, 2024 NSDUH](https://www.samhsa.gov/newsroom/press-announcements/20250728/samhsa-releases-annual-national-survey-on-drug-use-and-health)).

**Suicide rates rose and then stopped improving.** About 48,800 Americans died by suicide in 2024, an age-adjusted rate of 13.7 per 100,000. The rate climbed from 13.0 in 2014 to 14.0 in 2017 and has stayed roughly flat since then. Men die by suicide at about four times the rate of women (22.3 vs. 5.6) ([CDC NCHS Data Brief 572](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db572.htm)).

**Most U.S. suicides involve a gun.** In 2023, about 27,300 of roughly 49,300 suicides (55%) involved a firearm. That year also set a record for gun suicides ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-tank/2022/02/03/what-the-data-says-about-gun-deaths-in-the-u-s/); [Johns Hopkins](https://publichealth.jhu.edu/2025/new-report-highlights-us-2023-gun-deaths-suicide-by-firearm-at-record-levels-for-third-straight-year)).

**Teenagers are struggling.** In the CDC's 2023 survey of high school students:

- 39.7% reported persistent sadness or hopelessness. That includes 52.6% of girls.
- 20.4% seriously considered suicide.
- 9.5% attempted suicide.

([CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/su/su7304a9.htm))

**Overdoses are falling but still very high.** Drug overdoses killed 79,384 people in 2024, down 26.2% from 2023. That was the largest one-year drop on record. Even so, the 2024 rate of 23.1 per 100,000 was still well above the 2014 rate of 14.7 ([CDC NCHS Data Brief 549](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db549.htm)).

**There aren't enough providers.** At the end of 2025, about 137 million Americans lived in federally designated mental health professional shortage areas ([HRSA](https://data.hrsa.gov/default/generatehpsaquarterlyreport)).

## How does the U.S. compare?

**The U.S. suicide rate is near the top among wealthy countries.** The Commonwealth Fund compared 13 high-income countries using OECD data. Here is how they rank, from highest to lowest ([Commonwealth Fund, 2023](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/issue-briefs/2023/jan/us-health-care-global-perspective-2022)):

| Country | Suicides per 100,000 |
|---|---|
| South Korea | 24.1 |
| Japan | 14.6 |
| **United States** | **14.1** |
| Australia | 12.4 |
| Sweden | 12.4 |
| France | 12.3 |
| New Zealand | 12.1 |
| Norway | 11.8 |
| Switzerland | 11.1 |
| Canada | 10.5 |
| Netherlands | 10.0 |
| Germany | 9.7 |
| United Kingdom | 8.4 |
| *OECD average (38 countries)* | *11.1* |

*Rates are age-standardized. Most are from 2020, but some countries' figures go back as far as 2016.*

The U.S. ranks third of the 13, and its rate is about 27% above the OECD average. It is nearly 70% higher than the UK's. It is also well below South Korea's, so "the worst in the world" would be wrong.

**Americans want help but struggle to get it.** A 2020 Commonwealth Fund comparison of 11 high-income countries found:

- About a quarter of U.S. adults reported a mental health diagnosis or emotional distress. That was one of the highest rates of the 11 countries.
- U.S. adults were among the most willing to seek professional help for emotional distress.
- They were also among the most likely to report that care was hard to get or to afford.
- Only about one-third of U.S. primary care practices had a mental health professional on staff, compared with more than 90% in the Netherlands and Sweden.

([Commonwealth Fund, 2020](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/issue-briefs/2020/may/mental-health-conditions-substance-use-comparing-us-other-countries))

**Drug deaths are also near the top.** The same analysis found the U.S. had the second-highest rate of drug-related deaths among the countries studied.

**Spending isn't the problem.** The U.S. spends nearly twice the OECD average on health care as a share of its economy, yet had the lowest life expectancy of the 11 countries in that comparison ([Commonwealth Fund, 2020](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/issue-briefs/2020/jan/us-health-care-global-perspective-2019)). The issue is how the money is spent, not how much there is.

## How we could fix it

These are my own views, kept brief.

### 1. Start with family, faith and connection

Most of what protects people from despair happens at home, in congregations and among friends, long before anyone sees a doctor.

- **Loneliness is a health risk.** In 2023 the U.S. Surgeon General declared loneliness an epidemic. He found that social isolation raises the risk of depression and that its risk of early death is comparable to smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day ([AP](https://abc17news.com/news/ap-national-news/2023/05/02/loneliness-poses-risks-as-deadly-as-smoking-surgeon-general/)).
- **Marriage is protective.** A 20-year national study in Norway found that married people had lower suicide risk than people who were single, divorced or widowed, even after adjusting for income and education ([Næss, Mehlum & Qin, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8242039)).
- **Faith is protective.** Women who attended religious services weekly had about one-fifth the suicide rate of women who never attended ([VanderWeele et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2016](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/newsplus/church-attendance-may-lower-suicide-risk-in-women/)).

These studies show connections, not proof that one thing causes the other, but the pattern is consistent. Government should support families and partner with churches and community groups, not crowd them out.

### 2. Be realistic about guns

The idea of "banning" guns doesn't work in a country with more guns than people. Americans own an estimated 393 million firearms, about 120 for every 100 residents ([Small Arms Survey via World Economic Forum](https://weforum.org/agenda/2018/06/there-are-more-guns-than-people-in-the-us)). Since more than half of suicides involve a gun, the practical approach is to work with gun owners. The gun industry and suicide-prevention groups already partner to put prevention materials in gun shops and ranges ([NSSF](https://www.nssf.org/?p=12994)). We should expand that, and make it easy to leave a gun with a friend or a shop during a hard time.

### 3. Pay for results, not programs

Fund what works and stop funding what doesn't:

- **Collaborative care,** where a care manager and a psychiatrist support a patient's regular doctor, improves depression outcomes ([JAMA Psychiatry 2025](https://www.ajmc.com/view/collaborative-care-approaches-ranked-by-depression-outcomes-in-primary-care)).
- **It can save money.** In one trial, it lowered patients' total health costs by about $3,360 over four years ([Unützer et al., 2008](https://www.ajmc.com/view/feb08-2835p095-100)).
- **Results should be published.** Any program paid for with public money, including the 988 crisis line, should report its outcomes, and funding should follow them.

## The bottom line

Compared with other wealthy countries, the U.S. spends more on health care and gets worse results: one of the highest suicide rates, one of the highest overdose death rates and some of the hardest-to-reach mental health care. More spending alone won't fix that. Stronger families and communities, a realistic approach to guns and paying only for what works will.
